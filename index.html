Case note · Barramundi Lates calcarifer · 33 days post-hatch · 22 Oct 2026

Presumptive diagnosis

Viral nervous necrosis (Betanodavirus)

Five signs seen in this batch, an over-inflated round swim bladder above all, match the barramundi VNN picture in Australian diagnostic references. The hatchery has a history of nodavirus. Mortality has stayed under 5% and fallen for six days, a mild course that fits infection in older larvae. Bacteria found in the gut are treated as secondary.

BFull clinical picture Observed + references

Seen in this batch

  1. 1
    Swim bladder hugely over-inflated, large and roundnew finding

    Main internal sign of VNN in barramundi; it makes the larva buoyant. [1, 2]

  2. 2
    Floating at the water surface, head-up

    Loss of buoyancy control and equilibrium. [1]

  3. 3
    Pale body: clear skin with only black melanophore dots

    “Diseased barramundi become lighter”; siblings are pigmented all over. [1, 3]

  4. 4
    Body curved laterally, trunk to caudal fin

    Abnormal posture with loss of equilibrium; check whether the curve is fixed. [1]

  5. 5
    Internal organs visible through the body

    Pale, translucent body wall; gut and liver show through. [1]

  6. 6
    Low mortality, falling for 6 days

    Under 5% of the population, as in earlier episodes here. VNN mortality generally falls as fish age, and lesions are milder in older fish. [2]

Other VNN signs to check today

  • Spiral, looping or corkscrew swimming [1, 2, 3]
  • Erratic, uncoordinated darting; bursts of hyperactivity [1, 2]
  • Resting belly-up (loss of equilibrium) [1]
  • Head poking out of the water [1]
  • Stopped feeding; empty gut; lethargy [1, 2, 3]
  • Blindness: no reaction to light or feed [1]
  • Emaciation and skin abrasions [1]
  • More cannibalism on weak larvae [2]
Expected on histology

Vacuolation of brain and retina, most often in the optic tectum and cerebellum in barramundi. Not yet examined. [1, 2]

Why the history matters. Vertical transmission from broodstock during spawning is the most common route, and fish that survive an outbreak can carry the virus for 12 months or more. A recurrence in a hatchery with past nodavirus is therefore plausible. At 33 dph, past the 9–28 dph peak, milder disease and low losses are expected. [1, 2]

Co-factor to exclude

Nutritional deformity. Vitamin C-deficient diets cause caudal lordosis in barramundi. If the curve stays fixed after alizarin staining, check feed. [4]

Secondary only

Vibrio harveyi. High in the thesis guts (EW 41.56%, LW 59.35%, NW 5.49%) [8], but its fish signs are vasculitis, gastro-enteritis and eye lesions, not neurological signs. It can add losses in weak or abraded larvae. [5, 6]

Unlikely

Tenacibaculum. Needs mouth erosion, ulcers or fin rot; none seen. [7]

DNext steps

  1. Now
    Contain

    Isolate affected tanks. Stop sharing nets, water and equipment; disinfect between tanks. Remove dead and moribund larvae. Move no fish.

  2. Now
    Report the suspicion

    VER is a reportable disease in Australia. Contact the state hotline (Biosecurity Queensland) or the EAD Watch Hotline 1800 675 888.

  3. Now
    Sample before treating

    20 affected + 10 normal larvae. Whole larvae (<1 cm) or whole heads (1–5 cm) in RNAlater or 80–95% ethanol for PCR; whole larvae in 10% formalin or Bouin’s for histology; unfixed on wet ice (<24 h) for virus isolation.

  4. 24–48 h
    Confirm

    RT-qPCR or RT-nPCR is recommended for a presumptive result. Confirm with RT-nPCR + sequencing, or histology + IHC/IFAT showing vacuoles in brain and retina.

  5. 24–48 h
    Trace the source

    Identify the spawn and broodstock batch. Vertical transmission from broodstock is the most common route; survivors can carry virus for 12 months or more.

  6. 48 h
    Check co-factors

    Vibrio count on TCBS (secondary invaders). Alizarin red on fixed larvae to see whether the body curve is a true spinal deformity.

Antibiotics do not treat VNN. Use them only if a bacterial secondary infection is confirmed and a veterinarian prescribes them.
